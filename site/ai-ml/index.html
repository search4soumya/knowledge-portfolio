A model is one step in a pipeline. These notes follow the path from a decision you need, through data and evaluation, to a system that can be turned off safely.

Map of the work

Machine learning builds a function from examples instead of writing every rule by hand. Artificial intelligence is the broader aim: systems that take on tasks we associate with judgment. Most useful systems are narrower than the aim. They classify, rank, forecast, or generate, inside a boundary someone chose.

A model is that function plus its parameters. It is the middle of a pipeline:

  1. Decide the action the system may take, and the cost of being wrong.
  2. Collect examples that match the moment of decision, not a convenient export.
  3. Train, then measure against a baseline a person could explain.
  4. Serve the model behind the same standards as any other dependency: latency, failure, and a way to roll back.
  5. Watch the inputs. A model does not announce that the world moved.
If a short rule or a query already makes the decision well, a model is extra machinery. Start there. Add learning when the rule table is what keeps breaking.

Kinds of learning

The kind of learning follows from the feedback you have, not from whichever algorithm is popular this year.

Match the setup to the signal you can actually collect.
SetupThe signalTypical job
SupervisedEach example has a label or a target numberSpam or not, price, next value, "which class"
UnsupervisedNo labels, only the structure in the inputsGrouping, finding odd points, compressing
ReinforcementA reward after a sequence of actionsControl and game-like decisions, expensive to get right outside a simulator

A discriminative model scores or labels an input. A generative model draws new samples that resemble its training data. Generation is not understanding, and a fluent paragraph is not a checked fact. If the product needs a source of truth, put retrieval or a system of record next to the model and design for the case where they disagree.

Transfer learning starts from a model trained on a broad set and adapts it to a smaller one. It helps when your labeled set is thin and the broad model has seen similar inputs. It still needs an evaluation on your data. A leaderboard score is not that evaluation.

Data and features

Label quality beats model cleverness more often than notes like this admit. A clean definition of the label, written down, is part of the system. "Churn" that means three different things to three teams will train a model that is sure and useless.

Leakage

Leakage is when the training rows contain information that will not exist at decision time, or information that is the answer in disguise. A column copied from the outcome, a feature computed with the full future window, or a random split that puts the same user in both train and test: the offline score looks wonderful, and production looks ordinary.

Splits

  • Split by time when the world moves forward. Train on the past, test on a later period.
  • Split by entity when rows are repeated per user, device, or account, so the model cannot memorize an identity.
  • Random row splits are for independent rows. Most business tables are not independent rows.

A feature must be computable when you predict. If building it requires tomorrow's table, it is not a feature. It is a leak, or it is a batch job you have not scheduled. Document the freshness: how old the value may be, and what the model should do when it is missing.

Bias in the collection shows up as confidence later. Who is missing from the examples will be the group the metric does not describe.

Training and evaluation

Training fits parameters to a training set. Evaluation asks whether the fit helps on data the fit did not see. Report the second number. Keep a baseline next to it: the majority class, the current rule, or a small linear model. A complex model that does not beat the baseline has not earned its operational cost.

Metrics follow the cost of errors

  • Accuracy hides failure when one class is rare. A model that always says "no fraud" can be 99% accurate and never catch a case.
  • Precision is the share of alarms that were right. Low precision trains people to ignore the system.
  • Recall is the share of real cases you caught. Low recall is silent miss.
  • Calibration asks whether a score of 0.8 is right about four times in five. Rankings can look fine while the probability is fiction. Do not show an uncalibrated score as a probability.

Slice the metric. An average can rise while a region, a language, or a customer tier gets worse. The slice you refuse to measure is the one that will page you.

Offline evaluation is a filter, not a launch. When the cost of a wrong action is high, shadow the model on live traffic, or send a small share of decisions, before it acts alone. Watch the decision, not only the loss curve.

Serving

A trained file is not a service. Serving is an ordinary production problem with a versioned artifact in the middle.

  • Batch scores a table on a schedule. It fits when the decision can wait: nightly limits, morning lists.
  • Online scores one request inside a latency budget. It needs a feature lookup that is as fast as the model, and a timeout.
  • Fallback is the path when the model is slow, down, or unsure. A default rule, a previous score, or a human queue. Shipping without a fallback is shipping a hard dependency.

Version the model, the code that builds features, and the training data pointer together. A rollback that restores the model and leaves a new feature definition in place is not a rollback. Record the version on each prediction so a bad week can be traced.

After launch

Input drift means today's examples do not look like training. Prediction drift means the score distribution moved. Neither proves the decisions got worse, and a stable dashboard does not prove they stayed good. Keep a sample of outcomes, with a delay that matches how long the truth takes to arrive, and compare.

Cache a prediction only when the same inputs must yield the same action. Do not cache your way past a user who withdrew consent or a price that changed.

Judgment and limits

A model repeats patterns in the data, including unfair ones and outdated ones. It does not know your policy. Policy has to be written beside it: who may be scored, what the score may change, and which decisions stay with a person.

  • State the intended use and the uses that are out of scope. A model fit for ranking search results is not a model fit for denying a service.
  • Treat training data as data you are accountable for. Private text, customer records, and scraped content do not become harmless because a training job consumed them.
  • Generated text can be confident and false. If you display it, give the reader a way to see the source, or do not present it as a record.
  • Keep a person responsible for the decision the system informs. "The model said so" is not an owner.
  • Plan to turn the model off. If the business cannot run the fallback for a day, you do not have a model in the loop. You have a single point of failure with a research name.
Measure harm on the groups affected, not only on the average user. A small overall error can still be a large error for the people with the least room to absorb it.
